# Why Some Homeless People Have Better Hair Than You

Video script written in the QOVES Studio format. Target length: 11–13 minutes (~1,900 words of voice-over).
Angle: it isn't the lifestyle — it's the *absence of damage*. The practical takeaway is that you can get the same result with a low-intervention routine and a shampoo/conditioner that doesn't undo it.

---

## 1. Channel analysis — what QOVES does and why it works

**Positioning.** QOVES (Shafee Hassan) frames beauty as an *engineering + anthropology* problem. Every video is "here is a thing you noticed, here is the mechanism, here is what you can actually do." Never a vlog, never hype.

**Tone.** Calm, measured, second-person-plural ("if we look at…"). Confident but hedged where the evidence is thin. Cites studies by author/year on screen. Dismantles a myth mid-video to earn trust, then gives the practical protocol.

**Structure (almost every video):**
1. Paradox / counterintuitive observation in the first 10 seconds.
2. "To understand why, we have to look at…" → mechanism.
3. Three to five numbered factors, each with a visual comparison.
4. A myth-bust or "what most people get wrong."
5. "What this means for you" — a protocol, ranked by impact.
6. Soft CTA (analysis service / product), then a philosophical close.

**Visual language.** Black or off-white backgrounds, side-by-side portraits, thin red/white annotation lines, zooms into hair cuticle diagrams, slow push-ins, subtle grain. Text overlays are short nouns, not sentences.

**Title/thumbnail patterns.** "Why X Looks Y", "The Science of X", "X Is Making You Uglier", "Why Ugly People Look Attractive". Thumbnails: one face, one annotation, three to five words.

**Hooks the channel reuses.**
- The *contrast hook*: two people, opposite circumstances, unexpected winner.
- The *"you've been lied to" hook*: an industry incentive that makes you worse off.
- The *case-study hook*: one viral person, then the general rule.
- The *self-audit hook*: "check your own X right now."

**Scenarios / situations this video sits inside.**
- The viral "attractive homeless man" photos (Rafael Nunes in Brazil, 2013, is the best-documented; several others in Vietnam, the Philippines and the US).
- The "no-poo" / water-only movement and its partial truths.
- The post-2021 lawsuits over formaldehyde-releasing preservatives in mainstream shampoos.
- The growing distrust of "clean beauty" marketing — which is exactly why an ingredient-standard, not a brand claim, is the right close.

### ACT 1 — WHAT "GOOD HAIR" ACTUALLY IS (0:35–2:30)

**[VISUAL]** Cross-section diagram of a hair strand: cuticle (overlapping scales), cortex, medulla. Slow rotate.

**VO**
To understand this we need to be precise about what "good hair" means, because most people are judging the wrong variable.

Hair is dead tissue. It cannot heal. Every strand is a keratin cable wrapped in six to ten layers of overlapping scales — the cuticle. When those scales lie flat, light reflects off them in one direction, and you get shine. When they lift or chip, light scatters, and you get dullness and frizz.

**[VISUAL]** Electron-microscope images: healthy cuticle (smooth roof-tile pattern) vs. damaged cuticle (lifted, chipped). Labels: *INTACT* / *DAMAGED*.

**VO**
There's a second layer on top of that — a thin coating of sebum, the oil your scalp produces. Sebum is not dirt. It's a lipid film that fills the gaps between scales, repels water, and, at the right thickness, is the best conditioner ever made — because it was made for exactly this hair.

**[VISUAL]** Overlay text: *Hair = keratin + cuticle + sebum*. Then: *It cannot repair itself.*

**VO**
So "healthy hair" is really just hair where the cuticle is intact and the lipid film is present. That's it. And here's the key point: since hair can't repair itself, the healthiest hair isn't the hair that's been *treated* the most. It's the hair that's been *damaged* the least.

Which reframes the whole question. We shouldn't be asking what the man on the street is doing *right*. We should be asking what everyone else is doing *wrong*.

---

### ACT 2 — THE FOUR THINGS HE ISN'T DOING (2:30–7:00)

**[VISUAL]** Four-item list builds on screen: *1. Heat  2. Chemistry  3. Over-washing  4. Mechanical stress*.

#### Factor 1 — Heat (2:40)

**[VISUAL]** Thermal-camera footage of a flat iron. Temperature readout climbing past 180°C. Cut to microscope image of "bubble hair."

**VO**
Number one: heat.

Hair holds water inside the cortex. Push it past roughly 130 to 150 degrees Celsius and that water flashes to steam, expanding inside the fibre and leaving voids — dermatologists literally call it "bubble hair." Flat irons run at 180 to 230. Blow dryers held close run well over 100.

**[VISUAL]** Citation overlay: *Lee et al., 2011, Annals of Dermatology — hair-dryer temperature and distance vs. cuticle damage.*

**VO**
A 2011 study in the Annals of Dermatology looked at exactly this. Even a normal dryer, held too close, produced measurable cuticle damage. The undamaged control group? Hair that was air-dried.

Someone without access to electricity is running the control condition every single day.

#### Factor 2 — Chemistry (3:40)

**[VISUAL]** Animation: disulfide bonds inside the cortex, drawn as cross-links. Bleach animation snaps them one by one.

**VO**
Number two: chemistry.

Colour, bleach, relaxers, perms, keratin treatments. All of these work by breaking the bonds that hold the keratin together — and then, at best, partially rebuilding them. Bleach oxidises the disulfide bonds. Relaxers hydrolyse them. Keratin smoothing treatments, until very recently, relied on formaldehyde to re-set the fibre.

**[VISUAL]** Overlay: *Bond broken → cannot be fully restored.*

**VO**
None of that is fully reversible. You are permanently trading structural integrity for a temporary shape or colour. The people in these viral photos have virgin hair — every bond that was there at the follicle is still there at the tip.

#### Factor 3 — Over-washing with the wrong surfactant (4:40)

**[VISUAL]** Side-by-side: a lab beaker of sodium lauryl sulfate foaming aggressively vs. a mild glucoside surfactant with low foam. Then a strand diagram showing the sebum layer being stripped.

**VO**
Number three — and this is the big one — washing.

Most mainstream shampoos are built around sodium lauryl or laureth sulfate. These are excellent detergents. That's the problem. They're strong enough to strip the sebum film completely, and in doing so they lift the cuticle, which is why the "squeaky clean" feeling is actually the feeling of a roughened surface.

**[VISUAL]** Text: *Daily wash + strong surfactant = daily cuticle stress.*

**VO**
Do that every day and you're running a daily abrasion cycle on tissue that can't repair itself. Then, because the hair now feels dry and rough, you buy a conditioner, a serum, and an oil to put back a synthetic version of what you just removed. The industry sells you the problem and the solution in the same aisle.

**[VISUAL]** Myth-bust card: *"Your scalp over-produces oil to compensate."* — stamped *WEAK EVIDENCE*.

**VO**
Quick myth-bust, because this is where the no-poo crowd goes wrong. You'll hear that over-washing makes your scalp "overcompensate" and produce more oil. The evidence for that is weak. Sebum output is mostly driven by hormones, not by how often you wash. What actually happens is that stripped hair *shows* oil faster, because there's no even lipid film to absorb it. Different mechanism, same visible result.

The man on the street isn't washing daily with a strong detergent. His sebum film is intact, evenly distributed, and — critically — regularly rinsed with plain water, which removes dust and sweat but not lipids.

#### Factor 4 — Mechanical stress (6:00)

**[VISUAL]** Slow-motion footage of a brush pulled through wet hair; strands stretching, cuticle scales lifting. Overlay: *Wet hair loses ~30% of its tensile strength.*

**VO**
Number four: mechanical stress.

Wet hair is dramatically weaker than dry hair — the cortex swells, the cuticle lifts, and the fibre can be stretched past its recovery point. Brushing wet hair, rough towel-drying, tight elastics, and cotton pillowcases all abrade the cuticle. Repeated wet-dry swelling cycles even have a name: hygral fatigue.

**[VISUAL]** Back to the viral portrait. Annotations: *no brushing → no styling → no tension.*

**VO**
Somebody who isn't styling their hair isn't brushing it wet, isn't scraping it back, isn't towel-scrubbing it. Their hair is under almost no mechanical load. Over months, the difference is visible from across the street.

---

### ACT 3 — THE HONEST COUNTER-ARGUMENT (7:00–8:30)

**[VISUAL]** Tone shift. Neutral grey background. Text: *This is not a case for homelessness.*

**VO**
Now — we have to be honest about the other side of this, because it matters.

Homelessness is not good for hair. Chronic stress and malnutrition push follicles into their shedding phase — telogen effluvium. Sun exposure oxidises the cuticle. Cold, wind, and no access to clean water all take a toll. And the photos you've seen are survivorship bias: you noticed the one person in a thousand with striking hair, and never the rest.

**[VISUAL]** Overlay: *The lesson is not the lifestyle. It's the absence of damage.*

**VO**
So the lesson isn't "live rough." The lesson is that in a very specific window — genetically strong hair, no heat, no chemistry, no daily detergent, no mechanical stress — the *absence* of intervention beats almost any *amount* of intervention.

The question is whether you can recreate that window without recreating the circumstances. And you can. But not by doing nothing.

---

### ACT 4 — WHY YOU CAN'T JUST STOP WASHING (8:30–9:45)

**[VISUAL]** "No-poo" thumbnails montage, then a scalp close-up with product build-up and flaking.

**VO**
This is where people take the wrong lesson.

If you live in a city, sleep on a pillow, sweat at the gym, wear product, and have a normal to oily scalp, water-only doesn't produce the viral photo. It produces build-up. Sebum plus sweat plus dead skin plus airborne particulates forms a film that oxidises, smells, and feeds the yeast responsible for dandruff. Your scalp is a living organ with a microbiome; it needs to be cleaned.

**[VISUAL]** Diagram: scalp (living, needs cleansing) vs. hair length (dead, needs protecting).

**VO**
The distinction that resolves the paradox is this: the *scalp* needs cleaning. The *hair* needs protecting. A strong-surfactant shampoo does the first and destroys the second. Water alone does the second and fails the first.

What you actually want is a cleanser mild enough to leave the lipid film mostly intact — and, just as importantly, one that isn't quietly adding its own damage.

---

### ACT 5 — WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU: THE PROTOCOL (9:45–11:45)

**[VISUAL]** Ranked list builds, largest impact at top.

**VO**
So here's the protocol, ranked by how much each step matters.

**[VISUAL]** *1. Remove heat.*

**VO**
One. Remove heat. Air-dry. If you must use a dryer, keep it on the cool setting and at least fifteen centimetres from the hair. This is the single largest variable.

**[VISUAL]** *2. Stop the chemistry.*

**VO**
Two. Stop the chemistry — or if you colour, accept the trade and lengthen the intervals. Every process is a permanent withdrawal from an account you can't refill.

**[VISUAL]** *3. Wash less, wash gently.*

**VO**
Three. Wash two to three times a week, lukewarm, focusing the cleanser on the scalp and letting it run through the lengths. Rinse with plain water on the days between.

**[VISUAL]** *4. Choose a non-toxic, mild cleanser.* Ingredient list scrolls; certain names get highlighted red.

**VO**
Four — and this is where "gentle" and "non-toxic" become the same decision.

Look for mild surfactants: coco-glucoside, decyl glucoside, sodium cocoyl isethionate, cocamidopropyl betaine. Look for a formula at roughly scalp pH — 4.5 to 5.5 — which keeps the cuticle closed.

**[VISUAL]** Red highlights: *DMDM hydantoin · Quaternium-15 · Imidazolidinyl urea · undisclosed "fragrance"*.

**VO**
And avoid the ingredients that damage you in ways you don't see in the mirror. Formaldehyde-releasing preservatives — DMDM hydantoin, quaternium-15, imidazolidinyl urea — were at the centre of class-action lawsuits against major shampoo brands over scalp irritation and hair loss. Ethoxylated ingredients can carry 1,4-dioxane as a manufacturing contaminant. And "fragrance" is a legally protected blank cheque for dozens of undisclosed compounds, several of which are known contact allergens.

**[VISUAL]** *5. Condition the length, not the scalp. Protect it mechanically.*

**VO**
Five. Conditioner goes on the lengths only — it's replacing lipids where sebum can't reach. Then protect mechanically: squeeze, don't rub, with a microfibre towel. Detangle with a wide comb once the hair is mostly dry. Sleep on silk or satin.

**[VISUAL]** Return to the two portraits from the cold open. Left portrait's annotation lines now resolve into a single shine band.

**VO**
Do that consistently and you get what the man on the street got — an intact cuticle and an intact lipid film — without spending months growing out damage you didn't have to cause.
